01

Predict what matters, not every pixel

In short

World-action models turn video generators into robot policies by imagining the future and acting on it. But imagining every future pixel is slow, and it makes the policy care about things that don't matter for the task, like a shadow or a new tablecloth.

SLAP (Semantic Latent Action Prediction) keeps the video model's knowledge of how the world moves, but asks it to predict what the future scene will contain, in the feature space of a vision encoder, rather than how it will look. The result is a policy that holds up under visual change, learns real tasks from a few dozen demonstrations, and runs about as fast as a standard VLA.

02

Method

Start from a pretrained video model, which already knows how the world moves, and change three things to make it a good policy.

1

Semantic latents, not pixels

A robot needs to know what is where and how it moves, not the exact color of every pixel. Predicting meaning instead of appearance makes the policy shrug off lighting and background changes, and it takes one pass instead of a long denoising loop.

2

Read out from the middle

The last layers of a video model specialize in drawing pixels, which control doesn't need. Stopping partway through gives better features for acting and a model that is about half the cost.

3

Understand once, act many times

The scene is processed once and cached; the action head then refines its plan against that cache. Inference stays close to the cost of a plain policy.

04

What makes SLAP work

All variants share the architecture and action objective; we change one factor at a time. Numbers are LIBERO / LIBERO-Plus success (%), with the LIBERO-Plus breakdown per perturbation.

Supervision space: semantic latents beat pixels

Pixel-level video flow matching has the best clean LIBERO score but falls to 74.3 on LIBERO-Plus, collapsing under camera (50.9) and noise (72.0) shifts. All three semantic targets (DINOv3, SigLIP, V-JEPA) land at 80–82, so the gain comes from leaving pixel space rather than from any one encoder. An action-only model with no visual objective is more robust than pixel prediction, but loses 3 points on clean LIBERO.

Read-out depth: the middle layer is best

Reading out at layer 15 of 30 beats the final layer on both benchmarks while halving the backbone compute. The final layer (30) is increasingly specialized to pixel generation and is brittle to camera shifts (59.3), while an early layer (8) has not yet built enough scene structure for control.
